A few years ago I heard a radio interview on NPR with an author of a novel in which the narrator introduces each character in terms of three books that best describe him or her. I pondered this idea in search of three books that would form a composite image of who I am and what my values are. I selected Jane Austen’s *Pride and Prejudice*, Philip Hallie’s *Lest Innocent Blood Be Shed*, and Christopher Hill’s *Holidays and Holy Nights*.

## *Pride and Prejudice* by Jane Austen

Like Jane Austen, I am simultaneously a romantic and a pragmatist. I love a happy ending, but I realize that happy endings are often hard won. For better and for worse, one’s choices in life are shaped by numerous familial, societal, and financial considerations.

The novel’s hero, Elizabeth Bennet, possesses several qualities that I also see in myself. She is smart, clever, personable, persistent, and fiercely loyal. Through the course of the novel Elizabeth and Fitzwilliam Darcy discover that snap judgments can lead to false conclusions. Their original misunderstanding almost precludes them from discovering the common ground that ultimately transcends the class and status markers that divide them.

## *Lest Innocent Blood Be Shed* by Philip Hallie

Hallie’s researched account tells how a Huguenot congregation in southern France joined with its community during World War II to shelter Jewish refugees and help them escape to safety. Inspired by nonviolent resistance, Pastor André Trocmé, the Le Chambon congregation, and their neighbors organized a coordinated plan of care, forged passports, and built routes out of occupied Europe.

For me, Pastor André Trocmé and the congregation of Le Chambon embody what it means to live in the model of Jesus, carrying out the biblical commandment to love our neighbors as ourselves.

## *Holidays and Holy Nights* by Christopher Hill

Hill traces the development of the liturgical calendar in Europe, showing how cycles of nature and pre-Christian countryside traditions joined established Christian practices to shape the church year. He weaves together traditions surrounding Advent and Holy Week while introducing lesser-known observances such as Candlemas and Michaelmas.

Hill gently persuades readers to embrace the ways seasonal and Christian traditions can enrich spiritual life. His suggestions for activities and practices help individuals, families, and congregations integrate the rhythms of the liturgical year into daily life.
